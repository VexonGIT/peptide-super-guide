# Peptide Super File

Last reviewed: July 11, 2026

This file is for learning only. It is not medical advice. It does not tell you
what to take, where to buy it, how much to use, or how to mix it.

## The Short Version

A peptide is a short chain of amino acids. Amino acids are the small building
blocks that also make proteins.

Some peptides are real prescription medicines. Some are skin-care ingredients.
Some are research chemicals sold online with big claims. Do not treat all of
these as the same thing.

Use this rule:

- Green lane: FDA-approved medicine for a real diagnosis.
- Yellow lane: human research exists, but the product or use is not approved.
- Red lane: research-only, gray-market, or FDA safety concern.

## Big 2026 Updates

### 1. Compounded GLP-1 drugs are still a major safety topic

FDA says unapproved GLP-1 versions can be risky because they do not go through
FDA review for safety, effect, or quality before sale.

FDA also says compounded drugs should only be used when a patient has a medical
need that cannot be met by an FDA-approved drug.

Watch for these red flags:

- The seller says a compounded drug is the same as an FDA-approved drug.
- The seller does not require a licensed doctor.
- The label has spelling mistakes or a strange pharmacy address.
- The product arrives warm when it should be kept cold.
- The product says "research only" but is being sold for human use.

### 2. Retatrutide and cagrilintide are not normal compounding options

FDA says retatrutide and cagrilintide cannot be used in compounding under
federal law. FDA also says they are not parts of FDA-approved drugs and have not
been found safe and effective for any condition.

That does not mean the science is fake. It means they are not approved patient
medicines.

### 3. FDA has concerns about many popular online peptides

FDA lists several bulk substances used in compounding that may present
significant safety risks. These include names people often see online:

- AOD-9604
- BPC-157
- Cathelicidin LL-37
- CJC-1295
- Dihexa
- DSIP / emideltide
- Epitalon
- injectable GHK-Cu
- GHRP-2
- GHRP-6
- Ipamorelin
- Kisspeptin-10
- KPV
- Melanotan II
- MOTs-C
- PEG-MGF
- Selank
- Semax
- TB-500 / thymosin beta-4 fragment
- Thymosin alpha-1

The common FDA themes are simple: not enough human safety data, possible immune
reaction risk, impurity concerns, or serious adverse event signals for some
substances.

### 4. Semaglutide salt forms are not the approved drug ingredient

FDA says semaglutide sodium and semaglutide acetate are different active
ingredients than the one used in approved semaglutide drugs. FDA says it is not
aware of a lawful basis for using those salt forms in compounding.

### 5. Athletes need a separate anti-doping check

Some peptide hormones, growth factors, growth hormone axis drugs, and related
substances can be banned in sport. If you compete, check the current WADA list
and your sport's rules before using any product.

## How To Read Any Peptide Claim

Ask these five questions:

1. Is it FDA-approved for this exact use?
2. Is it being prescribed by a licensed clinician?
3. Is it filled by a real state-licensed pharmacy or made by the approved maker?
4. Is there human evidence, not just animal or cell data?
5. Are the risks and stop signs clear?

If the answer is "no" or "I do not know," slow down.

## Skin-Care Peptides

Skin-care peptides are usually a cosmetic topic, not a prescription drug topic.

Common examples:

- GHK-Cu / copper peptides
- palmitoyl pentapeptide-4
- acetyl hexapeptide-8
- palmitoyl tripeptides

Important difference: topical skin-care use is not the same as injectable use.
FDA flags injectable GHK-Cu in its compounding safety-risk page.

## A Simple Safety Checklist

Before trusting any peptide claim, ask:

- What exact product is this?
- Is it FDA-approved for this use?
- Who prescribed it?
- Who made it?
- Who dispensed it?
- Is the pharmacy real and state licensed?
- Does the label show the product name, strength, lot, storage, and expiration?
- What side effects should make me stop and call for help?
- What follow-up is planned?

If a seller cannot answer basic questions, that is a red flag.

## Bottom Line

Peptides are not one thing. Some are approved medicines. Some are cosmetics.
Some are experiments. Some are sold with claims that run ahead of the proof.

The safest way to read any peptide claim is:

Evidence first. Source second. Medical fit third. Hype last.
