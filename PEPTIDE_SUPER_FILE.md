## 1. The Short Version

A peptide is a short chain of amino acids. Amino acids are small building
blocks. Long chains of amino acids can make proteins.

Peptides are not all the same.

- Some are FDA-approved medicines.
- Some are cosmetic skin-care ingredients.
- Some are research drugs still being tested.
- Some are sold online even though safety and benefit are not proven.

Use this simple lane system:

| Lane | What It Means | How To Treat It |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Green | FDA-approved medicine for a real diagnosis | Use only with a clinician and real pharmacy. |
| Yellow | Human research exists, but the use is not approved | Interesting science, not a green light. |
| Red | Research-only, gray-market, or FDA safety concern | Slow down. Do not treat marketing as proof. |

## 2. Evidence Key

A strong body mechanism does not prove a real health benefit. A compound can
look good in cells or animals and still fail in people.

| Evidence Level | What It Means | Plain-English Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strong | FDA-approved use or several good human trials | We know more about benefits and common risks for a specific use. |
| Moderate | Multiple human studies, but limits remain | Useful evidence exists, but it may not fit every goal. |
| Limited | Small trials, regional studies, or mixed results | Possible benefit, but the answer is not settled. |
| Very low | Mostly animal, cell, or anecdotal data | Human benefit and long-term safety are unknown. |

### How To Judge A Claim

Ask these questions:

1. Was this exact compound tested?
2. Was it tested in people?
3. Was there a placebo group?
4. Was the study large enough and long enough?
5. Did it measure a real health result, not just a lab marker?
6. Were side effects reported?
7. Did another research group repeat the result?

## 3. Big 2026 Updates

### Compounded GLP-1 Drugs Are Still A Major Safety Topic

FDA says unapproved GLP-1 versions can be risky because they do not go through
FDA review for safety, effect, or quality before sale.

FDA says compounded drugs should only be used when a patient has a medical need
that cannot be met by an FDA-approved drug.

Watch for these red flags:

- The seller says a compounded drug is the same as an FDA-approved drug.
- The seller does not require a licensed doctor.
- The label has spelling mistakes or a strange pharmacy address.
- The product arrives warm when it should be kept cold.
- The product says "research only" but is sold for human use.

### Retatrutide And Cagrilintide Are Not Normal Compounding Options

FDA says retatrutide and cagrilintide cannot be used in compounding under
federal law. FDA also says they are not parts of FDA-approved drugs and have not
been found safe and effective for any condition.

That does not mean the science is fake. It means they are not approved patient
medicines.

### FDA Flags Many Online Peptides For Safety Concerns

FDA lists several bulk substances used in compounding that may present
significant safety risks. Names people often see online include:

- AOD-9604
- BPC-157
- Cathelicidin LL-37
- CJC-1295
- Dihexa
- DSIP / emideltide
- Epitalon
- injectable GHK-Cu
- GHRP-2
- GHRP-6
- Ipamorelin
- Kisspeptin-10
- KPV
- Melanotan II
- MOTs-C
- PEG-MGF
- Selank
- Semax
- TB-500 / thymosin beta-4 fragment
- Thymosin alpha-1

The common FDA themes are simple: not enough human safety data, possible immune
reaction risk, impurity concerns, and serious adverse event signals for some
substances.

### Semaglutide Salt Forms Are Not The Approved Ingredient

FDA says semaglutide sodium and semaglutide acetate are different active
ingredients than the one used in approved semaglutide drugs. FDA says it is not
aware of a lawful basis for using those salt forms in compounding.

### Athletes Need A Separate Anti-Doping Check

Some peptide hormones, growth factors, growth hormone axis drugs, and related
substances can be banned in sport. USADA has stated that BPC-157 was added to
the WADA Prohibited List under the S0 Unapproved Substances category in 2022.
The current official WADA list controls, so athletes should check WADA and their
sport rules before using any product.

## 5. Weight-Loss And Metabolic Medicines

### Semaglutide

Status: FDA-approved products exist.

Plain-English job: Copies a GLP-1 signal. It can lower appetite, slow stomach
emptying, and help blood sugar.

What we know:

- Approved semaglutide products have strong human evidence for labeled uses.
- Approved products are not the same thing as every compounded or online
  product.
- Common problems can include stomach side effects.

What to be careful about:

- FDA has warned about dosing errors with compounded injectable semaglutide.
- FDA warns that some semaglutide salt forms are not the approved active
  ingredient.
- Medical history matters, including thyroid cancer syndromes, pancreatitis,
  gallbladder disease, dehydration, kidney problems, and other drugs.

### Tirzepatide

Status: FDA-approved products exist.

Plain-English job: Works on GIP and GLP-1 pathways. It can affect appetite and
blood sugar.

What we know:

- Approved tirzepatide products have strong human evidence for labeled uses.
- The approved product and the label matter.

What to be careful about:

- Online "research" tirzepatide is not the same as an FDA-approved product.
- Combining incretin drugs is not a routine evidence-based shortcut.
- GI, gallbladder, dehydration, blood sugar, and stomach-emptying issues need
  medical follow-up.

### Retatrutide

Status: Investigational.

Plain-English job: Designed to act on GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon pathways.

What we know:

- Human trials are active and early results are interesting.
- FDA says retatrutide cannot be used in compounding under federal law.

What we do not know:

- It is not FDA-approved as of this review.
- Long-term real-world safety is not settled.

### Cagrilintide

Status: Investigational.

Plain-English job: An amylin analog being studied for appetite and weight.

What we know:

- It is being studied, including in combination research.
- FDA says cagrilintide cannot be used in compounding under federal law.

Bottom line: Research interest is not the same as patient access.

## 6. Growth Hormone Axis Compounds

The growth hormone axis affects growth hormone and IGF-1 signaling. This is not
a casual wellness lane.

Why this lane is tricky:

- It can affect blood sugar.
- It can affect fluid retention and joint symptoms.
- It may matter in cancer screening and hormone-sensitive conditions.
- A higher lab number does not prove a better health result.

### Tesamorelin

Status: FDA-approved for a narrow use.

Plain-English job: A growth hormone-releasing factor analog.

What we know:

- It is approved for reducing excess abdominal fat in adults with HIV and
  lipodystrophy.

What to be careful about:

- It is not a general weight-loss drug.
- The label and diagnosis matter.

### Sermorelin, CJC-1295, Ipamorelin, GHRP-2, GHRP-6

Status: Not approved as general wellness drugs.

Plain-English job: These are sold around growth hormone release claims.

What we know:

- Some can affect growth hormone or IGF-1 markers.
- FDA lists several GH-axis compounds with safety or quality concerns for
  compounded use.

What we do not know:

- Better sleep, fat loss, injury repair, or anti-aging claims are not settled
  just because a lab marker changes.
- Long-term safety is often unclear.

Bottom line: Treat GH-axis stacks as high-medical-complexity, not as routine
wellness tools.

## 7. Healing And Recovery Compounds

This is the category with the most online hype.

### BPC-157

Status: Unapproved.

Plain-English claim: Often marketed for tendon, gut, nerve, or injury recovery.

What we know:

- Most positive evidence is animal or preclinical.
- FDA says it has no or only limited safety information for proposed compounded
  routes and lacks enough information to know whether it would cause harm in
  humans.

Bottom line: No proven human tendon, gut, or nerve protocol.

### TB-500 / Thymosin Beta-4 Fragment

Status: Unapproved.

Plain-English claim: Often marketed for recovery and tissue repair.

What we know:

- Full thymosin beta-4 and marketed fragments are not the same thing.
- FDA lists the thymosin beta-4 fragment with safety information gaps and
  peptide impurity concerns.

Bottom line: Do not borrow claims from a related molecule and treat them as
proof.

### KPV And LL-37

Status: Unapproved.

Plain-English claim: Often marketed for inflammation, immune, gut, or host
defense support.

What we know:

- FDA says it lacks key human safety information for KPV.
- FDA lists LL-37 with safety gaps and nonclinical concerns.

Bottom line: Immune-related claims need extra caution.

## 8. Skin, Pigment, And Cosmetic Peptides

### Topical GHK-Cu And Other Cosmetic Peptides

Status: Cosmetic lane.

Plain-English job: Often marketed for skin appearance, hair, or repair signals.

Examples:

- GHK-Cu / copper peptides
- palmitoyl pentapeptide-4
- acetyl hexapeptide-8
- palmitoyl tripeptides

Important difference:

- Topical cosmetic use is not the same as injectable use.
- FDA flags injectable GHK-Cu for safety gaps.

### Melanotan II

Status: Unapproved.

Plain-English claim: Often sold for tanning, libido, or body composition.

What we know:

- FDA cites serious case reports for compounded Melanotan II, including melanoma
  and other serious events.

Bottom line: Do not treat online melanotan products like approved melanocortin
medicines.

### Afamelanotide

Status: FDA-approved narrow use.

Plain-English job: A melanocortin medicine for adults with erythropoietic
protoporphyria, a rare light-sensitivity condition.

Bottom line: This is not a general tanning product.

## 9. Mitochondrial, Longevity, Brain, And Sleep Compounds

### MOTs-C

Status: Unapproved.

Plain-English claim: Often marketed for mitochondria, exercise, or metabolic
health.

What we know:

- FDA says it has not identified human exposure data on drug products
  containing MOTs-C.

Bottom line: Interesting biology does not prove injected treatment benefit.

### Epitalon

Status: Unapproved.

Plain-English claim: Often marketed for longevity.

What we know:

- FDA says it has not identified safety information for proposed compounded use.

Bottom line: No proven anti-aging or lifespan result for general use.

### Dihexa

Status: Research-only.

Plain-English claim: Often marketed for brain repair or cognition.

What we know:

- FDA says it has not identified human exposure data for drug products
  containing dihexa acetate.

Bottom line: Research-only means research-only.

### Semax, Selank, DSIP

Status: Not FDA-approved.

Plain-English claim: Often marketed for mood, focus, anxiety, or sleep.

What we know:

- Some have regional or small-study interest.
- FDA lists safety information gaps for compounded use.

Bottom line: Do not treat small or regional evidence as U.S. drug approval.

## 10. Sexual And Reproductive Health

### Bremelanotide / PT-141

Status: FDA-approved narrow use.

Plain-English job: A melanocortin receptor agonist.

What we know:

- Approved for acquired, generalized hypoactive sexual desire disorder in
  certain premenopausal women.

What to be careful about:

- It is not approved for men.
- It is not a general sexual performance drug.
- Cardiovascular history and blood pressure issues matter.

### Kisspeptin-10 And Kisspeptin-54

Status: Research area.

Plain-English job: These relate to reproductive hormone signaling.

What we know:

- There is fertility and hormone-axis research interest.
- FDA lists Kisspeptin-10 among substances with safety concerns for compounded
  use.

Bottom line: Fertility research does not equal a general hormone hack.

### Oxytocin

Status: Prescription medicine for narrow uses.

Plain-English job: A hormone used in specific obstetric medical settings.

Bottom line: Social bonding, mood, or relationship claims are not the same as
approved medical use.

## 11. Stacks, Blends, And "Protocols"

A stack means using more than one compound. This can make the story confusing.

Why stacks are risky:

- You may not know which compound helped.
- You may not know which compound caused a side effect.
- Side effects can add up.
- Drug interactions can be missed.
- More compounds usually means more uncertainty.

Common online stack names may sound official, but a catchy name does not prove
human safety or benefit.

Safer rule:

Do not trust a stack unless each part has a clear reason, a clear source, a
clear safety plan, and a clinician who can explain why the combination makes
sense.

## 12. Mixing, Storage, And Injection Math

The attached PDFs include mixing and syringe math. This public guide does not.

Why:

- Small math errors can cause large dose errors.
- Some products need exact storage rules from the label.
- Sterility matters. A clean-looking vial can still be unsafe.
- A certificate of analysis does not prove sterility, stability, correct fill,
  safe storage, or clinical benefit.

Plain-English rule:

If a product needs mixing, injection, cold-chain handling, or syringe math, it
belongs under licensed medical and pharmacy guidance.

## 13. Monitoring Questions

There is no one lab panel that makes every peptide safe. Monitoring depends on
the exact product, diagnosis, medical history, and other medicines.

Good questions for a clinician:

- What diagnosis is this for?
- Is this product FDA-approved for that use?
- What benefits are realistic?
- What side effects are common?
- What serious risks should I know?
- What medical history makes this a bad fit?
- What medicines or supplements can interact with it?
- What labs or follow-up are needed?
- What symptoms mean I should stop and call?
- How do I report a side effect?

Class-specific topics to ask about:

- GLP-1 and incretin medicines: stomach symptoms, dehydration, kidney function,
  gallbladder, pancreas, blood sugar, pregnancy planning, and eye disease in
  people with diabetes.
- GH-axis compounds: IGF-1, blood sugar, swelling, joint symptoms, sleep apnea,
  cancer screening, and hormone-sensitive conditions.
- Melanocortin products: blood pressure, nausea, skin changes, and dermatology
  checks when pigment changes are involved.
- Reproductive hormones: pregnancy goals, fertility, hormone labs, clotting
  risk, and sex-specific risks.
- Immune or antimicrobial peptides: immune disease, infection risk, allergy,
  inflammation, and unknown long-term effects.

## 14. Red Flags And When To Get Help

Stop trusting the seller if:

- The product is sold as "research only" but includes human-use instructions.
- The seller says no doctor is needed.
- The label is missing the pharmacy, lot, strength, storage, or expiration.
- The seller says compounded means FDA-approved.
- The price is too good to be true.
- The product arrives warm, damaged, cloudy, leaking, or unlabeled.
- The seller pushes many compounds at once.

Get urgent medical help for:

- Trouble breathing
- Throat, lip, tongue, or face swelling
- Hives with breathing symptoms
- Chest pain
- Fainting
- Severe or lasting belly pain
- Severe vomiting or signs of dehydration
- Confusion
- High fever
- Spreading redness, pus, or severe pain at an injection site
- Sudden vision changes
- Any severe new symptom after a drug or injection

## 15. Glossary

Peptide: A short chain of amino acids.

Amino acid: A building block used to make peptides and proteins.

Protein: A large molecule made from amino acids.

Analog: A changed version of a natural molecule.

FDA-approved: FDA reviewed a specific drug for a specific use.

Off-label: A clinician uses an approved drug for a use not listed in the
approved label.

Compounded drug: A pharmacy-prepared drug for a patient-specific need.
Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved.

Research-only: A label that means the product is not being sold as a medicine.

COA: Certificate of analysis. It may help check identity or purity, but it does
not prove safety or benefit.

GLP-1: A hormone signal involved in appetite and blood sugar.

GIP: A hormone signal involved in insulin and metabolism.

GHRH: Growth hormone-releasing hormone.

GHRP: Growth hormone-releasing peptide.

IGF-1: Insulin-like growth factor 1, a growth-related signal.

HSDD: Hypoactive sexual desire disorder.

WADA: World Anti-Doping Agency.

## Bottom Line

Peptides are not one thing. Some are approved medicines. Some are cosmetics.
Some are experiments. Some are sold with claims that run ahead of the proof.

The safest way to read any peptide claim is:

Evidence first. Source second. Medical fit third. Hype last.
